EART-H

I, C. Ezra Stiles, peripatetic cartographer of the Golden Fountain on Kiliman, do here present to the reader a work that hath consumed the better part of my life and the whole of my wandering. I am, by vocation and by sacred duty, a mapper of the holy sites — those granite tablets, iron cubes, sandstone arches, and all the greater forms through which the power of RA doth flow into the world. It is the charge of my order to walk the breadth of EART-H, to find these sites, to record their aphorisms, and to fix their positions upon the map so that those who come after us may know where divine power dwelleth. This is no mere occupation. It is a calling that demandeth the whole of a person’s body and the whole of their mind, for the sites are scattered without regard for the convenience of travellers, placed as often at the bottom of a ravine or upon a wave-battered reef as in any place a sensible person might think to look.

Yet in the course of these long peregrinations I have come to understand that the map is not merely a record of where things are. It is a way of thinking about the world entire. For one cannot chart the position of a sacred site without understanding the mountain upon which it sits, and one cannot understand the mountain without knowing the forces that raised it from the deep. One cannot reckon the journey between two shrines without knowing the rivers that must be forded, the winds that drive the rains, the beasts that hunt the passes, and the settlements where a weary traveller may find sustenance and shelter. And so, by degrees and over many years, what began as a simple catalogue of holy places hath grown into something far more ambitious: an attempt to set down the whole of what I know about the world — its shape, its airs, its waters, its peoples, and the strange and marvellous forces that bind them all together.

I confess freely that such an enterprise is, in its grandeur, almost absurd. What right hath a single cartographer to speak of the origins of the world, of the movements of the moons, of the great currents of trade and migration that have shaped the lives of millions? The knowledge contained herein is of such bewildering variety — touching upon the shape of continents and the habits of insects, the mathematics of celestial orbits and the customs of fisher-folk, the logic of settlement and the mystery of birth — that no one mind can truly hold it all at once. It is as though one were asked to draw a map not merely of the land but of the wind itself, and of the thoughts of every creature that the wind doth touch. I have done my best, and I ask the reader’s patience with an old man who hath perhaps reached beyond his grasp.

What I can offer is the testimony of my own experience, for I have walked upon every continent save frozen Amazonia, and I have spoken with folk of every station and condition. I remember the morning I first beheld the great fundament of Centre, when the mists parted and the mountains rose before me like the spine of some sleeping titan, and I understood for the first time that the bones of EART-H were not laid down at random but with a terrible and deliberate art. I remember the night I lay upon the deck of a Tusquean merchant vessel and watched two moons cross paths in the sky, their mingled light turning the sea to a sheet of hammered copper, while the sailors muttered prayers and the rigging hummed with an energy that made the teeth ache. I have sat with the ice-bearded shepherds of Salmon and heard them reckon the rains by the colour of the evening clouds with an accuracy that would shame any scholar’s instruments. I have argued with the physicians of the Fingers over the nature of Confluence until dawn broke grey through the windows, and I have knelt before golden fountains whose aphorisms I can still recite from memory, though their meaning remaineth as opaque to me as it was the day I first read them aloud.

All of this — the mountains and the moonlight, the rains and the roads, the settlements that rise and the knowledge that fades — I have tried to gather into the pages that follow. I begin with what little is known of our world’s creation and the vanished entity that made it. I proceed to the substance of the land itself, then to the moons that govern our fortunes, the airs and weathers that clothe the world in its many garments, and the sacred sites that order the distribution of divine power. From there I descend to the particular: the rivers and capacities that determine where people may dwell, the growth of societies from wandering bands to settled cities, and the roads and sea-lanes that knit those cities together. I have attempted, though with the humility of one who knows his limitations, to render all of this upon a single map. And I conclude with what I have learned of the great mystery of Confluence, and a chronicle of events that is, I freely confess, still very much a work in progress — more a collection of fragments and travellers’ tales than a proper history, and one that I hope to improve upon considerably before my legs give out entirely.

Knowledge is a strange thing. It is at once the most solid and the most fleeting of possessions. A mountain may be mapped and its position fixed for all time, yet the understanding of why it stands where it does may shift with every generation of scholars. An aphorism carved in stone endureth longer than any kingdom, yet its meaning changeth with every soul who reads it. I have spent my life gathering facts — the position of a shrine, the depth of a river, the number of days’ march between two cities — and yet I find that the more facts I gather, the less certain I become of what they mean when taken together. The world is vaster than any book, and more subtle than any map. All I can do is set down what I have seen, as faithfully as my art and my memory allow, and trust that those who follow will see further.

The Secret Text

The EART-H project adopts a practice from a fantasy series that I enjoy. In that world reading is understood as a feminine art and it is the practice among men of means (who cannot read or write themselves) to have women read to them for pleasure and edification. All that patriarchy aside, what I like about the way it is represented in the story is that texts all have footnotes made by generations of female readers that offer extensive and learned commentary on the texts themselves. These footnotes are withheld from the oral presentation of the works and the men know nothing about them. The footnotes create an entire alternative way of experiencing the text and encompass debates on the origins, authenticity, and historical context of the main text. I adopt a version of this practice here with the main narrative of EART-H presented in the chapters and a hidden set of commentaries presented as footnotes at the bottom of each page. You can choose to read them or not as you see fit.
