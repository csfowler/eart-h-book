4  The Lunar System

4.1 Lunar Intensity

The planet EART-H possesseth a most singular geography, shaped not by the labours of men alone but by the great tides of energy that pour forth from its three moons. These moons–Io, Ganymede, and Deos by name–do circle the world in orbits of marvellous complexity, ever turning and crossing one another’s paths, so that the surface of the earth is never long at rest from their influence. And I have seen with mine own eyes how, where their light doth strike the ground, it chargeth plants and beasts and even the very stones of the field with a magical energy of wondrous potency, which the inhabitants have learned to harness for all manner of purposes both great and small.

4.2 The Three Moons

Now I shall speak particularly of each moon in its turn, for each doth emit a form of energy that careful practitioners–whom the wise ones of these lands call channellers–may bend to create the most astonishing effects. Yet these effects do wax and wane according to the nearness of each moon unto the surface of EART-H, and so the inhabitants have, by long study and observation, learned to track the moons with great diligence, as a mariner watcheth the stars.

4.2.1 Io - The Red Moon

Of the red moon Io I must speak first, for it is the most fearsome and changeful of the three. It followeth a path most steeply inclined against the plane of the world and moveth in retrograde–that is to say, against the natural turning of EART-H–so that it sweepeth across the heavens in a manner most dramatic, creating terrible variations in magical intensity from one latitude to the next. Many clerks and learned sages have told me that its energy is the energy of transformation and change, and I can well believe it, for I have seen its red light fall upon a quiet valley and set all things therein to strange and sudden alteration.

4.2.2 Ganymede - The Green Moon

The green moon Ganymede followeth a path of moderate inclination, and I have observed a most remarkable property of its magic: that it doth persist longer in inanimate objects than the enchantments of the other moons. Indeed, several species of plant have adapted themselves most cunningly to Ganymede’s pattern of movement, taking full advantage of the charge it bestoweth upon them to grow with great vigour during periods of peak exposure. This moon circleth the earth in a stately fashion, passing over the same point upon the surface roughly once in every seven days. For this reason the wise women do link Ganymede to growth, healing, and the natural magic that runneth through all green and living things.

4.2.3 Deos - The Blue Moon

Of the blue moon Deos I shall say that it possesseth the most stable orbit of the three and moveth most slowly across the firmament, so that it doth intersect more frequently with the light patterns of its sister moons. Its steadfastness maketh it the ideal instrument for calculating the passage of time, and for this reason the astrologers and seers of EART-H do associate it with time, memory, and the magic of divination–by which they seek to know what is hidden and what is yet to come.

4.3 Lunar Energy Distribution

Now it must be understood that the moons do possess dramatically different levels of power upon the surface, according to how far they stand from any given point at any given hour. For at any one time, a full half of the world lieth in darkness with respect to each moon’s light, and there may be places–as I have witnessed in my wanderings–where no moonlight falleth at all, so that the land is bereft of all celestial charge; and yet in other places, by the strange conjunctions of their orbits, the light of all three moons doth shine together with a power most formidable.

I tell you in good faith that this variability in magical power, brought on by the ceaseless shifting and interplay of the three moonlights, is among the most crucial matters for understanding the evolution of all life upon EART-H. For I have spoken with naturalists and beast-keepers in many provinces, and they all do affirm that different species–and indeed different individuals within a species–possess an affinity for one type of moonlight above the others. This affinity may be trained and strengthened over the course of a lifetime, yet with rare exceptions, the one who groweth strong in one form of light doth thereby limit their capacity to work with another. And in certain rare cases, which I have heard told by trustworthy witnesses, individuals of especially powerful affinity for one form of light may be positively weakened and made infirm by exposure to the others.

At the most primal level of the natural world, the highly adapted species do bestir themselves when their favoured moonlight shineth strongest upon the land, and go dormant–sheltering in holes and dens and the deep places of the earth–when they are at their weakest. The natural world hath devised an almost infinite number of stratagems by which species have seized upon advantageous niches through the exploitation of these power differentials, and I have seen with mine own eyes how in certain places the visible flora and fauna do change utterly from one phase of the moon to the next, as though one had stepped from one country into another entirely.

What is true for the plants and beasts hath also shaped the development of the sentient peoples. In some provinces, power structures have arisen upon collaborative models, reflecting the wisdom of communities that must hedge their fortunes against periods of weakness in one form of moonlight or another. Other societies, by contrast, have found means to specialize, building institutions linked to particular types of power; yet these societies do favour mobility as their chief protection during seasons of weakness. And I must here record a most extraordinary marvel: in at least one case, a company of powerful sorcerers hath produced a flying castle–a great stronghold borne aloft upon enchantment–that moveth across the landscape directly beneath the blue moon Deos, so that their particular form of magic is always at its maximum potency, despite the very slow pathway that the moon doth travel across the surface. I have not seen this castle with mine own eyes, but many credible travellers have sworn to me upon their honour that it is so.

The mathematics of the lunar system are a major area of study upon EART-H, for practitioners do seek most earnestly to predict the timing and intensity of moonlight at specific locations. The complexity of the interactions maketh this a task of fearsome difficulty, yet over the passage of many ages a number of heuristics have been devised that do allow for reasonably accurate predictions. These predictions are of the utmost importance for the planning of agricultural cycles, magical rituals, and even the campaigns of war–for no general of sound mind would march their army into battle without first consulting the lunar tables.

The power surges of the three moons are at the very heart of this project as it first took shape. I spend a lot of time in my classes thinking about how property rights shape the reality of our world. I have always wondered what the world would be like if it wasn’t so reliant on private property and the original idea for the moons was based on my wondering what it would take to make property rights a losing strategy for human settlement.

My thinking on this is drawn significantly from James Scott’s book Against the Grain (Scott 2017). In this book Scott argues that hunter gatherer societies were more resilient, had better nutrition, and spent less time laboring than agricultural societies in the early days of human plant cultivation. He argues that agriculture was not a priori superior to hunter-gatherer society, it was demonstrably worse, but that the hierarchy of the agricultural societies and the infrastructure of that hierarchy made them very effective at growth and better adapted to dealing with the needs of larger societies (especially the creation of institutions for managing internal conflict within larger and more complex socieities). Agriculture is not inevitable because it is better, it is inevitable because it expands better, keeps better records of its success, and gobbles up adjacent societies to function as labor in its production system.

A side note I want to explore later is the degree to which Scott’s compelling narrative is consistent with current views in Anthropology. Graeber and Wengrow’s The Dawn of Everything (Graeber and Wengrow 2021) is an example. They accept Scott’s demolition of agriculture-as-progress but reject any account in which hierarchy follows inevitably from grain, arguing instead that people cultivated casually for millennia without committing to it, and that some societies took up agriculture and then set it down again. The idea of theirs I find most useful here is seasonal dual morphology — societies that lived under a chief through one part of the year and dispersed into egalitarian bands in the next — a real ethnographic version of one of my imagined outcomes induced by the moons.

What is that imagination and how does it connect to moon energy? First, I am imagining species that adapt to moon cycles (more on this later in chapter 5), those species surge and wane creating irregular harvests that resist one of Scott’s preconditions for an agricultural hierarchy – predictable harvest size, time, and location. Second, moon energy on this planet conditions power and while one moon is dominant certain individuals will be strong, but those same individuals will be weak at other times when their moon is absent from the land. Control of land is ultimately about power, but if we cannot reliably guarantee the ability to control a particular piece of property, then why would we grow crops there? Someone could come along and simply take them at harvest time if it happens to fall at a moment when we are weak. The metaphor that strikes home for me here is the fact that strawberry plants need to be protected from chipmunks and rabbits at precisely the time of year when I am most busy. I only allocate the time to protect them one year in ten and so I almost never harvest berries. There is a particular chipmunk that rubs this in by leaving the partially eaten first berry of the year on my doorstep every May.

Anyway, in conditions of shifting power, property has to become more mobile I think. Settler colonialism begins in our own world when property rights forced the poorest to move elsewhere to find room to plant paired with the quest for power associated with the control of new lands by the already land rich. Perhaps in a society where control of land is less stable societies become more communal? In our world control of land generated wealth that was then available for use in trade. It returned rents that created generational wealth and long term inequalities that underpin almost every social injustice we have. What if land didn’t have this power? Would we have found other ways to create difference? This is one of the primary axes I want to explore in EART-H.

Anyway, what would it take for a world to hold off the development of property rights that stem, seemingly inevitably, from agricultural production as a dominant form of human organization? My answer? Highly variable power so that the warlord of one day would be an easy mark the next day. Interestingly, my original partners in imagining this world–notably including a sociologist/demographer–were highly skeptical that shifting power would be enough to disrupt property rights. I agree to the extent that I don’t think it would disrupt hierarchy, but whether that hierarchy would insist on property rights is, for me, an open question. A huge challenge of building this world is separating out the pieces that are truly deterministic (like the math for calculating moon orbits) and the pieces that only seem that way because it is what we are used to (back to LeGuinn and Jameson from chapter 1).
