2  Origins

Of all the chapters in this work, the one that follows is the most perilous, for it concerns matters that no living soul can claim to have witnessed. What I set down here is drawn from the oldest fragments of scripture, from the oral traditions of peoples who reckon their histories in song rather than ink, and from the speculations of scholars who have spent their lives sifting truth from legend. I have gathered these accounts as faithfully as my art allows, yet I must warn the reader that where the other chapters of this book rest upon the firm ground of observation — mountains I have climbed, rivers I have forded, cities whose markets I have walked through — this chapter floats upon the waters of conjecture, and the reader must judge for themselves how much weight it will bear.

2.1 In the Beginning…

Now it is to be known that, as far as the wisest clerks can reckon, the Univers[ity] was always in existence, having neither beginning nor end that any sage has yet discerned. Our story begins when the entity [Profes]Sor [Fow]Ler conceived of an idea most wondrous. And from that idea, and from the power granted unto Sor Ler, there were created hundreds of worlds, all of them different yet strangely similar one to another, as though fashioned by the same hand but with a different purpose in each fashioning.

EART-A and EART-B were the first to emerge from this great labour, and their stories are told elsewhere, for now. But EART-H, where our story takes place, was among the very first, and I say to you truly that the care of its design reveals the art of far more than a mere clockmaker. For Sor Ler appointed the entity called RA to oversee EART-H, and in the early days it is said by those who keep the oldest records that RA was ever present upon the land—building up the mountains with great force, filling the seas to their very brim, and shaping with patient craft the earliest creatures that did roam the world.

I have heard from many peoples in many lands the oral histories that have been passed down from generation unto generation, and they speak of late nights when the mottled face of RA could almost be perceived in the heavens, vast and terrible and full of purpose. Whether these stories be apocryphal or have, within them, a kernel of truth, none living can say with certainty. What is known, and upon this all the learned I have consulted do agree, is that centuries if not millennia have passed in which EART-H has not known direct action by RA.

And though some peoples do still burn offerings to this vanished entity, as I have witnessed with mine own eyes in the highland shrines and coastal temples alike, the world itself has moved on, playing out the magnificent experiment that this being orchestrated, with no understanding among any of its inhabitants of how or why it continues to run.

2.2 The Great Experiment

Now I shall speak of the great experiment itself, for its nature remains one of the chiefest mysteries of EART-H, and I have put the question to every wise one and learned clerk I have encountered in my travels. The scholars of many lands do hotly debate whether the complex interplay of the three moons was made intentional by design, whether the distribution of magical energy across the breadth of the planet serves some greater purpose yet hidden from mortal understanding, or whether EART-H has simply grown and changed beyond its creator’s original vision, as a garden left untended will put forth strange new shoots. But what is not in doubt among any who have eyes to see is that this is an experiment. For in my wanderings I have beheld with mine own eyes how the landscape is littered with dozens if not hundreds of idiosyncratic places and items that clearly have their origins elsewhere, being of a craft and substance not native to this world. Some of these places, like those that offer healing to the sick and the weary—and I have visited many such sites and felt their virtue upon my own person—are patterned with such regularity across the continents that some plan must assuredly be at work. Yet the nature of that plan remains opaque even to the most diligent of seekers. I have furthermore heard tell of numerous cults that have risen and fallen over the centuries, each purporting to predict the end of the experiment and thus the end of EART-H itself, but so far, praise be, the experiment continues at its own pace and with its own mysterious logic.

2.3 The Vanishing

I have consulted many ancient chronicles and spoken with keepers of the oldest libraries, and the historical records do suggest that RA’s presence upon EART-H began to wane approximately 2,000 years ago, though the exact reckoning of that timeline remains much disputed among the learned. The last confirmed manifestation of RA, so far as any trustworthy source can attest, occurred during what is now called the “Extension,” a most marvellous event in which several major magical artifacts were created simultaneously across different continents, as though the hand of the maker reached out in many directions at once before withdrawing forever. Now, while most processes upon EART-H seem to have proceeded without interruption since that time, I have journeyed to several parts of the planet where the living order of plants, beasts, and waters hath gone wild and strange in the absence of direct influence, like a horse that has thrown its rider and gallops where it will. These places draw both scholars and adventurers in great number, for their obvious flaws are thought to shed light upon the underlying logics of the experiment, and they do furthermore produce higher than average numbers of inexplicable phenomena—wonders and terrors alike that I have witnessed and shall recount in later chapters of this work.

2.4 Today

And now I shall tell you of EART-H as it stands in this present year of 2026, for I have seen much of it with mine own eyes and can speak with some authority. It is a complex and diverse world, marvellous in its variety, yet I say to you that it is characterized by chaos more than by order. The surges and ebbs of lunar power, governed by the three moons in their ceaseless dance, do make long-term projects most difficult to complete, and many a great work has been left unfinished when the tides of power turned against its makers. The societies of EART-H have adapted to this instability in various and cunning ways: some have embraced the nomadic life, carrying their households upon their backs and following the currents of power as a shepherd follows their flock; others have developed intricate systems of alliances and trade to mitigate the risks that attend such fluctuations. No one civilization holds dominion over the whole of the planet, and there remain vast reaches of land and sea that are but lightly settled, where a traveller may walk for days without meeting another soul. We cartographers, who seek the wisdom of the healing sites, have visited most corners of the world in our quest to unravel the order and disorder of that great system, yet I confess that many remote locations have seen fewer than a dozen visitors in centuries, and what marvels they contain remain unknown to all but the wind and the beasts.

This project has emerged from my desire to create a world in which I can explore ideas about power, identity, and social formation. I am increasingly intrigued by the literature on Futurisms, and the claim that emerges from that work that we have difficulty imagining just futures because we are so closely tied to the present and the institutions we already have in place. By creating a world with different rules; particularly related to property rights and racial identity, I hope to be able to explore these ideas in a way that is less bound by my own experiences.

Imagination is disciplined by what we take to be settled fact, so that the institutions we live inside come to feel like features of the world rather than decisions somebody made. Ursula Le Guin in her 2014 National Book Award speech says “We live in capitalism, its power seems inescapable — but then, so did the divine right of kings. Any human power can be resisted and changed by human beings” (Le Guin 2014). That sentence is more or less a guiding premise of this project and one that has been dear to me since I first read J.K. Gibson-Graham’s The End of Capitalism (As We Knew It) (Gibson-Graham 1996), which insists that the economy is not one totalising system but a field of practices we have simply learned to describe as one. Mark Fisher’s Capitalist Realism (Fisher 2009) is another version of the same; the argument that the horizon of the thinkable has closed, and that this closure presents itself to us as mere realism. Fisher opens by invoking the notable line that “it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism” — a sentence usually attributed to Fredric Jameson (though Jameson himself introduces the line with ‘someone once said…’ Jameson 2003). His Archaeologies of the Future (Jameson 2005) and others that I have been reading treat utopian writing as a diagnostic instrument; what we cannot imagine tells you what we take for granted (Muñoz 2009; Levitas 2013).

The Futurisms literature pushes this further, and they are the reason I am building a world rather than writing an essay about one. Kodwo Eshun (Eshun 2003) argues that the future is contested territory, already colonised by other people’s projections, so that imagining otherwise is a political act rather than a recreational one. Walidah Imarisha and adrienne maree brown (Imarisha and brown 2015) make the practical case that organising is science fiction — that anyone working toward a world which does not yet exist is already writing speculative fiction, whether or not they call it that. And Grace Dillon’s Walking the Clouds (Dillon 2012), which named Indigenous futurism, offers a concept I have found useful here: Biskaabiiyang, returning to ourselves — the idea that one cannot imagine forward until one has unlearned the frame one was handed. Arturo Escobar’s Designs for the Pluriverse (Escobar 2018) argues that design is ontological: in making a world we make the terms upon which it can be inhabited. That is what this is. I have changed two rules— what the incentives are for establishing private property and how the visible traits of identity are inherited— and the remainder of this book is an attempt to follow those two changes honestly wherever they lead.

Concurrently, I have become intrigued by the idea of storytelling as a way to teach students about geography and about the topics of population, economy, and institutions that I teach in my classes (Cameron 2012; M. Ortiz 2026; M. B. Ortiz et al., n.d.). I am increasingly aware of the limitations of lectures conducted in front of students who are only partially engaged. I worry about a world in which ideas are simply something that gets looked up or sourced from an AI instead of things that are chewed on, tested, and refined. My hope is that this project can serve as a starting point for a new way of teaching students–through stories and through play. My intent is to use these footnotes moving forward as a place to make connections between the academic literature that motivates both this project and the implementation here. Ultimately, I am not sure whether I am making a game, a book, or a syllabus. Perhaps all three.
