3  Physical Geography

3.1 The Fundament

The most ancient legends and such fragments of legends as survive in crumbling scriptoria do affirm that the whole of EART-H’s landmass was wrought by RA in a brief and terrible season known among the learned as “The Funding.” In our present age this hath led scholars and sages of every province to speak broadly of the stuff whereof EART-H is made as ‘fundament.’ I have spoken with explorers who have noted, as their forebears noted before them these many centuries, that EART-H is composed of most cunningly structured systems of mountains, rivers, valleys, and streams – formations whose character of rock and shape of land may persist in like fashion for miles upon miles and then, of a sudden, change utterly. These places of change are held by some to be holy, for in their careful study we might come to apprehend something of RA’s great design for EART-H. Whether this be true or no is a matter upon which I, a mere cartographer, can only wonder – but in this chapter I shall set forth the story of creation as the most reliable scholars have pieced it together from their divers enquiries….

3.2 In the beginning….

In the beginning – and this I have on the authority of the most venerable clerks and holy ones with whom I have taken counsel in my wanderings – there was naught but water. A great and featureless deep, stretching in every direction beyond the keenest eye’s reckoning. Whence this water came, or by what miracle the seas did not rise up and swallow all when vast continents appeared as if from nothing – these are mysteries that even the wisest sages bid me not to question. It simply was so, and so it remained.

3.3 The First Fundament

Now it is believed by all who have studied the matter – and I myself have heard it sworn upon by monks, marsh-folk, and mariners alike – that RA began the creation of EART-H’s fundament by fashioning anew some semblance of their own homeland and setting it at the very centre of this new and empty space. It is by reason of this position, and the centrality it holdeth in the myth of RA, that the landmass is called Centre; and the folk who dwell therein, among its high ridges and deep-cleft valleys, do attribute unto their land a holiness that no traveller can fail to mark upon their arrival.

3.4 Tusque

The second continent to rise from the Fundament – and I have walked its sun-warmed hills with mine own feet – was Tusque, a land of rolling downs and vast and generous plains. It is said among the learned that RA shaped Tusque to be a place of abundance, where olive trees and grapevines should adorn the hillsides and the soil beneath should be dark and rich beyond measure. How that fair intention fared once the planetary winds and rains began their relentless work is, in truth, a tale for another chapter; but the promise of Tusque was sweet indeed.

3.5 Salmon

The third land to emerge from the Fundament was a wild and fearsome country of steep canyons and sheer precipices, cleft asunder by an enormous river the like of which I have seldom seen in all my travels. This river, which folk call the Great Divide, is said to have been carved by the very tears of RA as they wept for the world they had wrought. The peoples of this region depend upon that mighty watercourse for their very survival, and its fish – unusually robust and plentiful beyond all expectation – do sustain a far denser pattern of settlement than the high elevation and parched landscape would otherwise permit. I have myself seen the salmon leap in the shallows, silver-flanked and numberless, and marvelled at the providence thereof.

3.6 Then in quick succession….

And then – as I have been told by the most trustworthy chroniclers, and as the ancient texts do attest with one voice – new continents appeared in swift succession, each as if snatched bodily from some other world entire and set down upon the waters alongside those already there. Some were mountainous and terrible to behold, their peaks lost in cloud; some bore the marks of ancient ice, glaciated and scoured; others were flat and unadorned as a monk’s table. Yet each possessed its own peculiar beauty, and the world of RA’s imagination was truly a wonder such as no traveller could see without their heart being moved thereby…

3.7 The final pieces fall

And now I come to the last and grandest act of RA’s labour, which the sages say came when their resources were nigh unto spent, or when some cosmic balance was at last achieved. For RA’s final contributions were two vast continents, each far greater in extent than any that had come before. First there appeared a massive polar land, ringed along its southern border by mountains so impossibly high that none I have spoken with claimeth to have seen their summits clear of cloud. Next – and almost as if RA had resolved at the very last moment that there must needs be more trees and more lakes upon the face of EART-H – a huge granite massif rose from the deep, covering much of the Northeastern quarter of the planet with its dark forests and shining waters.

3.8 The World So Far

And so, as RA’s great work continued, the Fundament did take its shape, transforming from that endless and primordial ocean into a world of wondrous diversity – of mountains and valleys, of rivers and broad plains. With 80.45% of its surface yet covered by water, EART-H now possessed ten distinct continents, each bearing its own character and countenance, fashioned by divine intention and by those geological processes which the scholars of our age do continue to study and dispute amongst themselves. I, C. Ezra Stiles, have set foot upon every one of these lands, and I tell you truly: each is a world unto itself.

3.9 The Shaping of the Margins

Yet the work of creation was not complete with the mere placement of land upon the waters. For where continents lay close upon one another, or where the deep sea met abruptly with high cliffs of stone, the fundament was rough and ill-joined, as a mason’s wall before the mortar is set. And so there came a further shaping – a gentling of the margins, as the learned scholars describe it – whereby the boundaries between land and sea were given their proper shelving, and the places where one fundament abutted another were knit together with more natural gradation. This was, perhaps, the final act of RA’s geological craft: the smoothing of seams, the laying of continental shelves beneath the shallows, and the ensuring that water, when it fell as rain upon the high places, might find its way downward to the sea without impediment.

My design for this section was drawn in part from the work of Ross Thorn whose presentation at NACIS circa 2018 gave me the idea for creating a fantasy landscape by twisting and altering real digital elevation data. Accessing the data is relatively easy it turns out, but not every place makes for a good continent. Then, once data is downloaded the real problems begin. Chief among these is dealing with movement of large landmasses across the dateline, and particularly in the polar regions.

One of the things I am most interested in is how these places appear when they are out of scale and facing very different climate conditions. Will an experience of the Finger Lakes be different when they are steep inlets to the sea? How will the region around the River of No Return Wilderness be experienced when it is placed up near the North Pole?

Why real terrain and not noise

The obvious way to build a fantasy world is to generate the terrain procedurally (e.g. rough shapes sketched, mountains and rivers by feel, then some fractal displacement to add detail and make it not look quite so clunky). I chose not to for several reasons. The first is mechanical; Real landmasses have real hydrology and reflect the geologic and hydrologic processes that have shaped them. The second has to do with the act of imagination that I want to bring to this project. When we take something real and familiar and make it into something completely different we begin the process of re-thinking what we know about that place. The continents here are mostly places near and dear to me (with a scattering of places that fit a need – looking at you Canada, Amazonia, and Kiliman). The act of imagination I want to invoke in myself and in the readers of this book is one that immediately takes the familiar and, literally, turns it on its head.

The cost is that I inherit the landscape’s real history whether I want it or not. By making this a story about RA placing continents and Stiles’ observation that junctures are places that shed light on RA’s plan I am trying to make a strength out of something that is actually a weakness– when Centre juts into Tusque and the junction produces breaks in hydrology, radical shifts in elevation, and general chaos it becomes the storyline rather than a failure of my method. Splendidly, this resonates with early work in geography by some of the greats. Hepach, quotes Livingstone (who I read in my 1st semester of grad school) who is writing about early work in geography saying that for early geographers (in this case Carl Ritter) “To study geography was to explore nothing less than the very laws of the Creator, who was the author of the human story, the architect of the world-plan, and the builder of humankind’s earthly home.”(Hepach 2023, 468; citing Livingstone 1992, 141).

I highly recommend Hepach’s work who sees similar views in the writings of Alexander von Humboldt (“the external world has no real existence for us beyond the image reflected within ourselves through the medium of the senses. As intelligence and forms of speech, thought and its verbal symbols, are united by secret and indissoluble links, so does the external world blend almost unconsciously to ourselves with our ideas and feelings. (Humboldt, 1849 [1845]:59, emphasis by author)”(Hepach 2023, 469)), and especially Carl Sauer.

Sauer’s The Morphology of Landscape (Sauer 1925), another 1st term reading in grad school and something I also read as an undergrad, is famously understood as the origin of cultural geography – the belief that we can understand place as the outcome of the intermixing of culture and natural area. Sauer is still revered by geographers today as an antidote to the racist environmental determinism that plagued the earliest instances of the discipline (but see Duncan’s critique, also used in grad school to teach me the meaning of ‘reification,’ Duncan 1980). That viewpoint is important here because the world I will describe in subsequent pages is initially deterministic down to the placement of roads and the size of settlements, but because this world is meant to be played, the tableau I set here is essentially the natural landscape with the cultural landscape still to be imagined. Simple, definitely reifying, but still open-ended to the human imagination. We leave terrestrial geography for a moment and fly to the moon(s).
