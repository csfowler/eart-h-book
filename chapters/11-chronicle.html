12  A Chronicle of EART-H

12.1 Being a True Account of the Marvels and Wonders of This World

In which the reader shall find recorded the histories, great events, and curious occurrences that have shaped the lands and peoples of EART-H, set down in the manner of those learned travellers who have walked these strange shores and witnessed with their own eyes the workings of this most peculiar experiment.

I, who have wandered far upon the face of EART-H and spoken with many clerks, mariners, marsh-folk, and wise ones of strange provinces, do here set down an account of the great changes that befell the world in elder days, as it was told to me and as I myself beheld in part. I call it true, and I mean by that word something narrower than the reader may hope. Nobody now living saw the most of what follows. What I have is what folk say, and where two peoples say different things I have written down both, and where nobody could tell me anything I have left the place empty rather than furnish it. My own profession has a long and not wholly honourable history of furnishing empty places, as the reader will learn before the end, and I would not add to it if I can help it.

12.2 Of the Funding

In the darkest recesses of the oldest tales of the most ancient peoples there is a time that every province calls by the same name, the Funding, when RA came down to EART-H and lived upon its surface. I have written in the first part of this book of what the learned make of RA. Here I set down only what the tales say of the folk who lived in RA’s time, for there were folk then, and they were not as we are.

They are called the First Folk, and they came, the tales say, to tend the earth and to live in harmony with the sea. They have since passed on. I asked in a great many places where they went, and was answered in a great many ways, of which the commonest is a shrug.

12.2.1 Of the First Mark

Before ever there was speech between the First Folk and the creatures of the world, there was a meeting of another kind. In some green and sheltered place, which every people I have asked describes with the same fond vagueness, as a place where nothing was wanting, a child was begun near a bird, and was born bearing something of it. This was the first mark, and it was the beginning of Confluence in the world, which I have treated at length in an earlier chapter and shall not treat again.

It was a cruel gift. The child bore the bird’s mark and carried with it a longing for flight, which none of the Folk could ever have, and which no mark since has granted either. Bird-marks were rare in those days and are rare now, and the reason the old folk give is a plain one: the birds were too quick to fly off. A beast that will stand in a field while a child is begun beside it leaves its mark on the world. A bird does not stay.

12.2.2 Of the First Cult of the Bird

Out of that longing came the first cult I have any record of. A company of the First Folk went uphill, first into the foothills and then into the high peaks and the cliffs where the great birds of prey nest, so that the children they begat should be begun as near to those birds as might be. They were possessed by Confluence as some are possessed by drink. They lived sparely and badly in the freezing cold of the heights, and they waited, generation upon generation, for something that never came, and they are a byword still. When a mother in the hill country of Kiliman sees her child set upon some single object to the ruin of everything else, she says that the child has gone up to the eyrie. I have heard it said of scholars and of lovers, and once, to my face, of a cartographer.

12.2.3 Of the First Speech and the Sundering

The First Folk could speak with all the creatures of the earth and the sea. Upon this every tale agrees, and it is the thing the tales are fondest of: that once a body might stand upon the shore and hold converse with a whale, or ask of a hare the way home.

That speech was broken. After the Sundering the Folk could no longer speak with the creatures of the earth and sea, and many of them could no longer speak with one another, so that neighbours who had been neighbours all their lives stood at their doors and could not make themselves understood. What broke it the tales do not agree upon, save in this: it was the new star in the sky that broke it, for the star came first and the silence after. So I was told in four provinces, and I have no reason to doubt it.

12.2.4 Of the Close Call

Then came the disaster, which the old folk call the Close Call, and which I would call by a heavier name if it were mine to call. A new star appeared in the heavens, and grew, and was a comet, and almost everybody died. I have heard the dying of the world told in a dozen ways, by fire and by water and by a great wind and by a silence, and I believe the tellers chose each according to what they themselves most feared.

When the comet had passed, there were the three moons in the sky. Only a very few survivors came through into this new world. Of what the sky held before the Close Call the tales say little, and of what it held after they say everything, for every reckoning of time that I know of begins there. The reader will remember that in the third part of this book I wrote of the moons as though they had always been. I leave both accounts standing. I have never been able to make the old folk of any country choose between a thing that was made and a thing that arrived, and I do not see why I should be held to a higher standard than they are.

12.3 Of the Age of the Archaic Library

Next was the Age of the Archaic Library, which stood upon the shore of the world like a second mountain, one of stone and thought. It was gathered from all the quarters of EART-H, and therein were books of histories and manners, of the courses of the stars, of medicines and engines, and also of sorceries and hidden arts, in numbers beyond counting. Its halls smelled of dust and vellum and old glue, and when one walked within, the sound was like a soft rain, being the whisper of pages stirred by unseen airs.

This Library was founded by one Joy Ball, a person of such exceeding wealth that the like thereof had not been known before. Some say this treasure was won by trade, others by cunning, and others yet by fortunes fallen from the heavens; but all agree that Joy Ball poured gold upon the world as one pours water upon a thirsty field, and thereby raised the Library stone by stone. As the wise ones of that land do say, Joy Ball believed that knowledge, once gathered, would endure longer than any lineage of rulers. In this Joy Ball was wrong, and the rest of this chapter is in some measure the account of how.

12.3.1 Of the Bookworms

No work of mortals is free from corruption. There arose within the Library certain creatures called bookworms, though they were no common worms but beings touched by magic. They were pale and soft, with faint letters glimmering along their skins like dew upon silk. These creatures gnawed not only paper but the very words themselves, so that as they fed they drew into their bodies some portion of the sense and learning of the books. A worm that had eaten histories would twitch as though remembering battles, and one that had fed on charms would glow faintly at night. Many clerks strove to destroy them, but the worms hid deep in the stacks, and their numbers were beyond counting.

Of one strange matter concerning these worms I heard from a fisher, whose name is now lost, but whose tale is repeated along the coasts in a vague and sketchy form that I take to be its honest one. On the eve of the Library’s destruction, when the air was heavy and the sea drew back as though inhaling, this fisher was hauling empty nets and cursing their luck. The librarians, labouring to save what they could, pressed upon them a crate of books already ruined by worms. As the fisher loaded the crate, they saw one bookworm still living, creeping slowly, its body marked with half-eaten lines of verse.

The fisher, having caught nothing all day and not being one to waste bait, took the worm and set it upon a hook. Straightway there came up from the deep an ancient fish, vast and grey-green, with eyes like cloudy moons. This was the Ganymede Sturgeon, of which it is said there is but one at a time in the world, and that it lives so long that nobody has ever seen one die. The fish swallowed the worm, and with it, as folk believe, a great hoard of the Library’s lost knowing. The fisher, trembling with awe, let the creature go, and it sank again without a sound. Most of what the worms had eaten was lost in the flood that followed. Some, if the coasts are right, lives yet, not in books but in a fish.

12.3.2 Of the Fall of the Library

Now it came to pass that the three moons of EART-H aligned, which is a thing so rare that none then living had seen its like. Their pull upon the seas was dreadful, and the tides rose and fell with a roar like beasts in pain.

Cassandra, who was learned in signs and dreams, had foreseen the doom of the Library, and she gave warning to all who would hear her. She laboured to save what she could, up and down the stairs with her arms full of books, and if the effort had gone on to its end a good deal more of the world’s plain knowledge might be with us now. It did not go on. She quarrelled with her lover over a matter that nobody remembers, and in the heat of it she was pushed, and fell down the stone stairs, and died. The work stopped with her. Thus, as often happens in the world, great losses were born of private folly.

The practitioners of magic, who had trusted Cassandra’s vision and did not stop to mourn her, acted swiftly. They drew the core of their lore into a place not of this world, folding it away as one folds cloth, so that the flood could not reach it. But the histories, the sciences, the plain knowledge of folk, were left behind. When the great wave came, green and foaming and loud as thunder, it struck the Library and carried it away, stones grinding like teeth, pages dissolving into the sea. That is why, to this day, it is easier to find a person who can work a charm than one who can tell you who built the bridge they are standing on.

12.4 Of the Age of Inundation

After this began the Age of Inundation, when knowledge was scattered like wreckage and lay unevenly upon the world, so that folk knew much in one place and nothing at all a valley over.

In the tidal margins, where fresh rivers meet the salt sea, there arose a bloom of living things so small that a thousand of them might swim in a spoonful, which the marsh-folk name the green shine. They glowed faintly green and gold, and when stirred they hummed softly, like a wire drawn tight. These creatures held a kind of power not born of spellcraft but of the meeting of waters, and in great quantity. The marsh-folk learned to gather this virtue, bottling light and motion, and by it they made wheels turn and lamps burn without fire. Their huts became foundries, their boats became engines, and the smell of rot and brine was joined by that of hot metal and oil. They praised themselves for their cleverness, saying that magic was no longer needed.

But beneath the waters lived the Leviathans, vast beings older than the Library itself. They fed upon the green shine in great draughts, and it filled them with a terrible vigour and a strange inventiveness. They built cities of coral and stone, singing as they worked, and their realm reached from the tidal edge down into the black cold places where light fears to go.

Fear grew among the marsh-folk. They said that the Leviathans would eat all the green shine and leave them nothing; and worse, they learned that the bodies of the Leviathans held a power greater than the shine itself. So they made war. As the elders of the marsh tell it, the weapons were made of barnacles. The green shine was poison to those clinging shells, and the marsh-folk learned to fix that poison and turn it into engines that drained and unmade the Leviathans’ power, so that a Leviathan struck by one would dim, its songs faltering, its vast body going slack.

The marsh-folk say the Leviathans were hunted until none were left, and the sea grew quiet. I set that down as they say it. The reader will see in the next part that it cannot have been wholly so, and that what the marsh calls the end of the Leviathans was rather the end of their being free.

12.5 Of the Great War

Of the Great War as a whole I was able to learn much less than I wished. Nobody I met could tell me when it began, nor whether those who fought it called it a war at all, nor how it was ended; and the songs of it, which are many, are all about its beginnings and its one great battle, as though the folk who made them could not bear to sing the middle. What I could gather, I give.

12.5.1 Of the Pelagiaic Temple

First, and spreading like wildfire from small beginnings, there was the Pelagiaic Temple. Its teaching joins together the tales of the First Folk with the tales of the Leviathans, and holds that the two were once one people, or near it, before the Sundering broke their speech. It teaches that folk must live according to the rhythms of the moons and not against them, and that the moons’ rough hand upon the world may be softened by those who do; and it teaches that by drawing near to the Leviathans again folk may return to the purer state of the First Folk, who could speak with every creature.

The Temple was founded in the memory of Mary of Lorflock, whose story I tell below, and her likeness is in every one of its houses that I have entered. I have heard its hymns sung in three tongues, and in all three the same name is the refrain.

12.5.2 Of the Lament for the Leviathans

Then came the Lament. It was not one people but a company gathered from all over EART-H, of every kind of folk and every manner of mark, who held that the hunting of the Leviathans was a perversion and meant to punish those who did it. They were joined by grief and by a fervour for a better world, and they are reckoned one of the first sparks of the Great War. I have met descendants of the Lament who are proud of it and descendants who will not speak of it, and in more than one family both.

For the hunting had not stopped, whatever the marsh-folk say. The Leviathans that were left had been taken alive and penned, and their power drawn from them, as one draws water from a well that is also a prisoner.

12.5.2.1 Of Mary of Lorflock

This is the tale the Lament tells of itself, and I have heard it told more often than any other in this chapter, most memorably by a teller in a harbour tavern who had it from a teller before, and who did the voices. I give it more plainly than that teller did, though I have kept the one or two lines that every telling keeps.

Lorflock was a poor town on a poor coast. The moons kept its seasons unreliable and its harvests uncertain, and its folk lived by growing radishes and putting them up, and lived on the margin of that. Into this town came one Master Vont with a Leviathan and a bargain to offer. Penned in an enclosure at Lorflock and drawn upon, the beast’s power would steady the seasons, and with steady seasons the town might have what it had never had: a harvest that could be counted on, land that could be handed down from parent to child, a future longer than next year. Vont meant to grow rich by it and did not hide the fact, and meant also, I think honestly, to make of Lorflock a true town at last. It was terrible for the Leviathan, Vont allowed, but it was for the good of so many. The beast was called, in the tongue of that coast, He-Who-Swims-Deep.

The town divided over it in the way towns do, which is to say mostly in silence. Sargon, who was Chief of the council that governed Lorflock, held that the place had always lived by its own ways and wanted no such creature in its harbour. Zeke, who worked double shifts at the radishes to keep himself and his daughter fed, had no time to hold any view at all. And his daughter, Mary, who was twelve years old, went down to the enclosure every day she could.

The town said Mary was touched. She had begun to know things she had no way of knowing, and when she was asked how, she said the Leviathan had told her. Her friend El, a boy of about her age, believed her. Her teachers complained that she would rather teach the class than sit in it. Her father heard only that she was missing her work and running with a bad friend, and feared the worst a tired parent fears; and Sargon, hearing the same reports, feared something stranger, for knowledge that comes out of a beast’s mouth is arcane knowledge, and the council had no wish to see that taken up in Lorflock.

The day the tale is about began with El caught at the enclosure, and Vont’s guards gathered, and half the town come down to the shore to see. There, before everyone, the quarrel that had been kept quiet came out loud. El said that they had only been trying to get near the beast, and that Vont did not know what it told Mary, and was hurting it. Vont answered that the creature was intelligent, and that “its sacrifice is all the greater” for that, and asked them all to look at how pitiful their town was. Sargon took that ill, and said so. Zeke told Vont that there had been folk come to slaughter the beast outright and Vont might have let them through and spared everyone this.

While they argued, Mary had gone into the water. El saw her first, a small head far out, swimming for the Leviathan. And Vont, who had been all reason until then, became frightened, and the tale makes much of it: the girl, Vont cried, was carrying the Tannenhauser Key, and if it reached the beast it could undo the bindings that held it (which the tellers call plasmic, a word I could get no one to explain), and the whole town would be levelled. “Men, retrieve that child!” Vont shouted. Zeke was already in the water after her. Vont, seeing the rescue for what it was, ordered the guard to kill them all, and Sargon told the guard that anyone who killed a townsperson that day would answer for it.

Mary turned the Key in the beast’s bindings, and the Leviathan went free. In its going it swallowed Mary and her father both, and every teller I have heard insists that it did not mean to. The Temple holds that it carried them living into the deep, and that Mary speaks there still with He-Who-Swims-Deep in the old tongue of the First Folk. The teller in the tavern held that too, and then added, more quietly, that it was a big fish and a small girl.

12.5.3 Of the Hegemonies

Out of the capture of the Leviathans came the first towns that did not live from one bad year to the next. A town with a Leviathan in its harbour could steady its seasons, and a steady town could make alliances, and the alliances became the first beginnings of empire. Among the commanders of that age one Rafina, called the Grand Hegemon, took an ocean-going fleet of merchants and made of it the first realm that was a realm in both senses at once, governing and making war, and bound a great number of the Leviathan-towns together under that one hand. It was the first time upon EART-H that one command held a host of that size. The towns that were not Rafina’s sought out one another in turn so that they might resist, and that is how nations began to be. I give this account with some unease, for it means that the realms of the world were founded upon creatures in pens, and I have never found anyone in any capital willing to say so in those words.

12.5.4 Of the Great Desertion

The Leveth were a wandering people who had found a comfortable valley, and had come, over some generations, to spend more and more of the year in it. There was quarrelling among them between those who would keep moving and those who would stay, and it was nearing a breaking point when the armies of the war began gathering near the valley and threatening it. That settled the matter, and gave the whole argument to those who would wander. It was expected that the Leveth would take their part in the war; they would not, having no wish to fight for Rafina, and instead they left. It is called the Great Desertion by those who expected them, and by the Leveth, I am told, something else that I was not told.

12.5.5 Of the Battle of Many Many Armies

The Great War ended, or came to its height, in the Battle of Many Many Armies. The name is said that way everywhere, with the word doubled, as a child says a thing that is too large for one word, and I have come to think the grown folk keep it for the same reason. It was the greatest battle of the age. Of who fought in it, and where, and who held the field at the end, I have heard a great many accounts and believe none of them sufficiently to set it down. Every people I asked had an ancestor there, and none had an ancestor who lost.

12.6 Of the Lost Time

12.6.1 Of the Forgetting

In the age after the war there were towns and many people upon EART-H, and in certain regions of the world, for reasons that no one knows, everybody forgot what had happened to them in the previous year of their lives. Not a year of the world’s history, which folk might have recovered from one another, but the last year of each person’s own life, in every person, all at once.

I have tried to make the reader feel this and cannot, so I will say only what I was told by a very old woman in a hill town, whose grandmother was a child in it. Her grandmother’s mother, she said, woke in a house she had never seen, with a child at her breast she did not know, beside a person who said they were her husband, and who was weeping because he did not know her either.

12.6.2 Of Christaller’s Rise

After the Forgetting, folk began to remark upon certain objects and small monuments that they could not recall having been there before, and they came to feel that these must somehow have appeared during the year that nobody remembered. The monuments had words carved upon them. Folk learned before long that with care and study those words could be read aloud to call forth powers, chiefly powers of healing and of growth.

These were the sacred sites, of which I have written at length in the seventh part of this book. Why the age is named for one Christaller I could never discover. Some say that Christaller was the first to read a shrine aloud and be healed; some that it is merely the word for the arrangement of the sites, as the old tile at the head of that chapter would suggest; and the scholars of Tusque, who make a calling of disagreeing with Kiliman, hold that the Extension of which I wrote in the first part of this book and the Forgetting are one event, told by two peoples who did not compare notes. I record all three, and note against my own interest that the third would make the sites younger than my fountain’s scholars like to believe.

12.6.3 Of the Unmarked Children

There was a generation of children whose births no one remembered and whom no one had wanted. They bore no mark at all, for whatever had stood nearby at their beginnings had stood there in the lost year, and nobody could tell them what it was; and perhaps for that reason they were drawn to the strange shrines, and spent their days at them. The unmarked are rare in any age. In that one there were a great many, all at once, and the world did not know what to do with them.

12.6.4 Of the Long Recovery

Then followed years in which folk tried to understand what had happened to them, and could not. They had babies. They were in houses they had not owned and in places they had never known. It took years for them to work out what had happened and what it meant, and the mark of that age is a great overturning of families and of private lives, which showed itself in turn in a great breaking of the old order: the councils, the guilds, the forms of marriage and inheritance, the manner in which poems were made and pictures painted. I have seen pictures from before the Lost Time and after it, side by side in a collection at the Golden Fountain, and a child could tell you which were which.

12.6.5 Of the Itinerant Empiricist Bards

Out of that need arose a calling. The itinerant empiricist bards went from place to place, observed everything they could, and told each town the story of what they judged had happened there in its lost year. Some leaned more toward the empiric and some more toward the bard. But folk found great comfort in having a story settled, and took up the bards’ stories wholeheartedly to fill in the gaps, and many a town’s history to this day is a bard’s tale, believed.

These were the first cartographers. We do not say it often outside the fellowship, and I say it here with some discomfort, for I have spent this whole chapter warning the reader against folk who fill empty places, and I find that I am descended from them.

12.6.6 Of the One Who Remembered

There was among those bards one called Gnostic, who went from place to place telling folk the stories of their lost year like the rest. Gnostic’s stories were fictions. But Gnostic, alone of anyone I have heard tell of, remembered what had truly happened. He knew what had befallen his own town, and from that he could reckon what had likely befallen others; and he knew, as he told his stories, that what he told was not wholly true.

Gnostic took an apprentice from among the unmarked children, a girl with a gift for metre and for noticing, and taught her what had really happened. Her name is not remembered, which some find fitting. It is a belief of my fellowship, held quietly and not much examined, that the true history of the Lost Time came down through her line to the cartographers of later days, and that is why we are fed in every village we enter. I have never seen any proof of it and have never met a cartographer who wished to look for any.

12.7 Of the Age of Sail

12.7.1 Of the Coming of the Sail

Folk had boats and rafts from the oldest times. But in this age there came great ocean-going ships that made long voyages carrying great cargoes, and did not hug the coasts; and this changed everything about how far a thing could be carried, and how much of it.

12.7.2 Of the Fell Tides

The first mariners to go far from land found soon enough that the three moons pulled the sea’s currents about and bewildered every reckoning they had. A great many ships were lost before the navigators understood the tides, and the understanding was got by trial and by drowning, and at the end of it they could make charts. The port-folk still say of any change that nobody saw coming that it is a fell tide.

12.7.3 Of the Salty Dogs

There have always been wandering peoples upon EART-H, and when the great ships came, some of them saw in the sea a new road to wander on. They did not settle in the harbours. They took to vast ships and lived on them whole, every household and generation, always upon the waves; and because they so seldom touched land they grew strange and closed in upon themselves. At first they traded as anyone trades. But after a few generations there were among them folk who were very proud never to have set foot upon land at all, and those of their company who had fallen into disgrace were sent ashore to deal with the unclean dirt-folk. I have been one of the dirt-folk, in a harbour on Salmon, and was dealt with by a sullen young person who had been sent ashore for some offence they would not name. We got on well enough.

12.7.4 Of the Moon’s Tongue

The Salty Dogs went everywhere, and some of them went far into the north, and came upon a hard and rocky country that nobody had known of. That is Amazonia, of which I wrote in the ninth part of this book, and which the roads have never reached.

The ship-folk sing of a thing they found in those waters which they call the Moon’s Tongue: a long tongue of warm water, and at times they say hot, running out into the northern sea from that country as a river runs, and reaching far into the cold. Along its edges, where the warm and the cold meet, they say there are living things to be found nowhere else, strange and many, that flourish in that border and die on either side of it. I have this from the songs and from one old sailor who said that their grandmother had dipped her hand in it. Nobody I have met went up it to its source, the Salty Dogs least of all, since that would mean setting foot upon land; and the reader will recall that in the fourth part of this book I wrote of a hot river said to run in that country’s interior, which I had from people who had likewise never been. Whether the songs and the rumour describe one thing I cannot say, and I am no nearer being able to say it than I was then.

12.7.5 Of the Trade Cities

In the harbours there grew up great cities, with many who lived in them always and many more who came and went, and these knitted the whole world together in one web of trade, from the sea-harbours to the inland parts of every continent, leaving out only the most remote places. The roads and harbours I described in the ninth part of this book are, for the most part, the bones of that web.

12.7.6 Of the Last Composition

Near the end of the Age of Sail, when that way of life was already passing and folk could feel it, a composer whose name I have been given in three ports, three different ways, wrote her last great opera, which is called The Fell Tide. She is remembered for two things. The first is her operas. The second is that she was the first to build companies of players that lasted, and were not wandering troupes but settled in the great port cities, each with its own house. They prospered because they helped folk forget that the whole of their way of life was coming to an end.

The Fell Tide is the story of a boy of the Salty Dogs who falls in love with a girl of the land, and of their parents, who forbid it. It is from this opera, as every port-dweller will tell you, that we have the saying about a fell tide; and if you hear it sung in Kiliman’s harbour at the end of a season you will understand why the saying stuck.

All these things I have written as faithfully as I could, as I heard them and as the signs of the world yet show. Some of it is surely wrong, for I had it from bards and sailors and from my own fellowship, who are all of them in the business of making a good story of a gap. If any doubt it, let them travel, and ask, and see, and write down what they are told, and know that they are now one of us.

This chronicle is a narrative summary of events in the EART-H world, and it is the one chapter of this book whose contents I did not decide. The underlying history was built across several sessions of Ben Robbins’s Microscope (Robbins 2011), a game in which players construct a history by jumping about inside it — establishing a period, then a scene within it, then a moment within that, in no particular order and with nobody holding the whole shape. What comes out has real gaps and real disagreements in it, which is the reason I use it rather than writing the history myself.

The text above was then generated by AI from those session summaries and rewritten into the voice this book uses throughout. I have edited it only lightly.

Two notes for anyone attempting the same. First, the voice here is not mine, and it is not a real medieval writer’s either. It is a pastiche of Mandeville — whose own Travels were themselves largely assembled out of other people’s books by a writer who very probably never went to most of the places he describes. A borrowed voice reporting a borrowed history struck me as the right amount of unreliability for a chapter of this kind, and I have left its oddities standing rather than smoothing them out. The chronicle above contradicts my own earlier chapters in at least one particular that I know of. I have decided this is correct.

Second, a chronicle is not yet a history. It becomes one when somebody imposes a shape on it — decides what caused what, and where the story starts and stops. Mary Campbell has done work on the travel-writing tradition Mandeville belongs to (Campbell 1988). And my friend Mona Domosh is part of a wonderful debate on the significance of travel writing that is still fresh in my mind even though I haven’t revisited it since grad school — her case for a feminist historiography of geography (Domosh 1991b), Stoddart’s objection that women travellers were producing something other than geographical knowledge (Stoddart 1991), and her reply that the frontier he was defending had been drawn to exclude them in the first place (Domosh 1991a).
