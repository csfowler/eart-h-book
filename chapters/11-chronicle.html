12  A Chronicle of EART-H

12.1 Being a True Account of the Marvels and Wonders of This World

In which the reader shall find recorded the histories, great events, and curious occurrences that have shaped the lands and peoples of EART-H, set down in the manner of those learned travelers who have walked these strange shores and witnessed with their own eyes the workings of this most peculiar experiment.

I, who have wandered far upon the face of EART-H and spoken with many clerks, mariners, marsh-folk, and wise ones of strange provinces, do here set down a true account, as it was told to me and as I myself beheld in part, of the great changes that befell the world in elder days. And though some matters may seem marvelous to those of narrow belief, I record them plainly, for so they were, and so the folk of those lands do yet affirm them.

12.1.1 Of the Age of the Archaic Library

First was the Age of the Archaic Library, which stood upon the shore of the world like a second mountain of stone and thought. This Library was gathered from all the quarters of EART-H, and therein were books of histories and manners, of the courses of the stars, of medicines and engines, and also of sorceries and hidden arts. Its halls smelled of dust and vellum and old glue, and when one walked within, the sound was like a soft rain, being the whisper of pages stirred by unseen airs.

This Library was founded by one Joy Ball, a person of such exceeding wealth that the like thereof had not been known before. Some say this treasure was won by trade, others by cunning, and others yet by fortunes fallen from the heavens; but all agree that Joy Ball poured gold upon the world as one pours water upon a thirsty field, and thereby raised the Library stone by stone. As the wise ones of that land do say, Joy Ball believed that knowledge, once gathered, would endure longer than any lineage of kings.

Yet no work of mortals is free from corruption. For in those days there arose within the Library certain creatures called bookworms, though they were no common worms but beings touched by magic. They were pale and soft, with faint letters glimmering along their skins like dew upon silk. These creatures gnawed not only paper but the very words themselves, so that as they fed, they drew into their bodies some portion of the sense and learning of the books. A worm that had eaten histories would twitch as though remembering battles, and one that had fed on charms would glow faintly at night.

Many clerks strove to destroy them, but the worms hid deep in the stacks, and their numbers were beyond counting.

Of one strange matter concerning these worms I heard from a fisherman, whose name is now lost, but whose tale is repeated along the coasts. On the eve of the Library’s destruction, when the air was heavy and the sea drew back as though inhaling, this fisherman was hauling empty nets and cursing his luck. The librarians, in their haste and terror, pressed upon him a crate of books already ruined by worms, saying he might use them for fire or ballast. As he loaded the crate, he saw one bookworm still living, creeping slowly, its body marked with half-eaten lines of verse.

Being a practical man, he took the worm and set it upon his hook. Straightway he cast his line, and there came up from the deep an ancient fish, vast and grey-green, with eyes like cloudy moons. This was the Ganymede Sturgeon, of which it is said there is but one at a time in the world, and that it remembers all waters it has swum since the beginning. The fish swallowed the worm, and with it, as folk believe, a great hoard of the Library’s lost knowing. The fisherman, trembling with awe, released the creature, and it sank again without sound. Thus some learning yet lives, not in books, but in living flesh.

12.1.2 Of the Fall of the Library

Now it came to pass that the three moons of EART-H aligned, which is a thing so rare that none living then had seen its like. Their pull upon the seas was dreadful, and the tides rose and fell with a roar like beasts in pain. Cassandra, a woman learned in signs and dreams, foresaw the doom of the Library and cried out to all who would hear her.

She labored to save what she could, running up and down the stairs with arms full of books, her hair loose, her breath sharp with dust and ink. But sorrow came from a small and bitter cause: she quarreled with her lover over matters now forgotten, and in the heat of words she was pushed, fell down the stone steps, and died. Thus, as often happens in the world, great losses were born of private folly.

The practitioners of magic, who trusted Cassandra’s vision, acted swiftly. They drew the core of their lore into a place not of this world, folding it away as one folds cloth, so that the flood could not reach it. But the histories, the sciences, the plain knowledge of folk, were left behind. When the great wave came—green, foaming, loud as thunder—it struck the Library and carried it away, stones grinding like teeth, pages dissolving into the sea. The air stank of salt and torn leather, and the cries of those watching were swallowed by the wind.

12.1.3 Of the Age of Inundation

After this began the Age of Inundation, when the lands were broken and memory itself became uneven. Knowledge was scattered like wreckage, and folk knew much in one place and little in another.

In the tidal margins, where fresh rivers kissed the salt sea, there arose a bloom of living things so small that a thousand of them might swim in a spoonful, which the marsh-folk name the green shine. They glowed faintly green and gold, and when stirred they hummed softly, like a wire drawn tight. These creatures drew forth a kind of power not born of spellcraft, but of the meeting of waters. The people of the marshes learned to gather this virtue, bottling light and motion, and by it they made wheels turn and lamps burn without fire.

Thus the marshdwellers grew mighty. Their huts became foundries, their boats became engines, and the smell of rot and brine was joined by that of hot metal and oil. They praised themselves for their cleverness, saying that magic was no longer needed.

But beneath the waters there lived the Leviathans, vast beings older than the Library itself. They fed upon the green shine in great draughts, and it filled them with a terrible vigor and a strange inventiveness. They built cities of coral and stone, singing as they worked, their voices rolling through the deep like distant drums. Their realm stretched from the tidal edge down into the black cold places where light fears to go.

Fear grew in the hearts of the marshdwellers. They said the Leviathans would eat all the green shine and leave nothing. Worse still, they learned that the bodies of the Leviathans held a power greater than the green shine itself. So they made war.

As the elders of the marsh do say, the weapons were born of barnacles. For the green shine was poison to those clinging shells, and the marsh-folk learned to fix and transmute that poison into devices that drained and unmade the Leviathans’ power. When struck, a Leviathan would dim, its songs faltering, its vast body going slack.

Thus were the Leviathans hunted and slain, until none were left. The sea grew quiet. And though the marshdwellers prospered for a time, many now say that the world has been poorer ever since, and that something bright and thinking was extinguished beneath the waves.

All these things I have written faithfully, as I heard them and as the signs of the world yet show. And if any doubt them, let them travel, and ask, and see. For EART-H is wide, and its wonders are not done.

This chronicle is a narrative summary of events in the EART-H world, and it is the one chapter of this book whose contents I did not decide. The underlying history was built across several sessions of Ben Robbins’s Microscope (Robbins 2011), a game in which players construct a history by jumping about inside it — establishing a period, then a scene within it, then a moment within that, in no particular order and with nobody holding the whole shape. What comes out has real gaps and real disagreements in it, which is the reason I use it rather than writing the history myself.

The text above was then generated by AI from those session summaries and rewritten into the voice this book uses throughout. I have edited it only lightly.

Two notes for anyone attempting the same. First, the voice here is not mine, and it is not a real medieval writer’s either. It is a pastiche of Mandeville — whose own Travels were themselves largely assembled out of other people’s books by a writer who very probably never went to most of the places he describes. A borrowed voice reporting a borrowed history struck me as the right amount of unreliability for a chapter of this kind, and I have left its oddities standing rather than smoothing them out. The chronicle above contradicts my own earlier chapters in at least one particular that I know of. I have decided this is correct.

Second, a chronicle is not yet a history. It becomes one when somebody imposes a shape on it — decides what caused what, and where the story starts and stops. Mary Campbell has done work on the travel-writing tradition Mandeville belongs to (Campbell 1988). And my friend Mona Domosh is part of a wonderful debate on the significance of travel writing that is still fresh in my mind even though I haven’t revisited it since grad school — her case for a feminist historiography of geography (Domosh 1991b), Stoddart’s objection that women travellers were producing something other than geographical knowledge (Stoddart 1991), and her reply that the frontier he was defending had been drawn to exclude them in the first place (Domosh 1991a).

Microscope produces the chronicle. The AI pass, and my editing of that pass, is where the shape gets imposed. I have tried to impose as little as I could stand to, and I am aware that “as little as I could stand to” is not the same as none.
